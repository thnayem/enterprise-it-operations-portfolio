# IT Team Leadership & Service Operations

Reliable IT operations depend on clear ownership, sensible prioritization and consistent communication. A team lead should make work visible, remove blockers and ensure that incidents, routine tasks and improvement work are followed through.

This guide describes practical operating habits. It does not claim a specific team size, formal management title or approval authority.

## Work intake and prioritization

Use one agreed channel for requests and incidents wherever possible. Record the service, impact, urgency, requester, owner and next update.

A practical priority model considers:

- **Critical:** major service outage, security incident or business-critical process unavailable.
- **High:** significant user or site impact with no reasonable workaround.
- **Medium:** limited impact or degraded service with a workaround.
- **Low / planned:** routine request, access change, maintenance or improvement.

Use the organization's approved severity and SLA definitions. Priority should reflect business impact, not simply the seniority of the requester.

## Daily team rhythm

- Review open critical and high-priority incidents.
- Confirm task ownership and due dates.
- Identify maintenance, site visits and supplier dependencies.
- Check pending approvals and change windows.
- Agree who communicates updates to users and stakeholders.
- Hand over unresolved work with the next action clearly stated.

## Incident and escalation ownership

The incident owner coordinates progress even when another engineer or vendor performs the technical work. Keep a clear timeline, communicate impact and next update, and confirm service restoration with the affected owner.

For recurring or high-impact incidents, document root cause, corrective action and preventive action. Track follow-up items to closure.

## Knowledge and handover

- Maintain runbooks for recurring tasks.
- Document changes and known dependencies.
- Use peer review for high-risk changes.
- Keep credentials in approved secret-management systems, never in shared notes or public repositories.
- Record shift handovers with current status, risk, next action and contact point.
- Cross-train team members on critical services to reduce single-person dependency.

## Service review measures

Use a small set of meaningful measures and explain what each one means:

| Measure | What it helps assess |
|---|---|
| Incident volume by priority | Demand and service pressure |
| Mean time to acknowledge | Responsiveness |
| Mean time to restore | Service recovery performance |
| Repeat incident rate | Effectiveness of corrective action |
| Change success / rollback rate | Change quality |
| SLA achievement | Service commitment performance |
| Backlog age | Workload and unresolved demand |
| Preventive maintenance completion | Operational discipline |
| Asset and documentation accuracy | Control quality |
| User feedback | Service experience |

Do not optimize a metric in isolation. For example, closing tickets quickly is not a good result if issues recur or users have not confirmed restoration.

## Stakeholder communication

For a significant incident or planned change, communicate:

1. What service is affected.
2. Who or what is impacted.
3. What is being done.
4. Whether a workaround exists.
5. When the next update will be provided.
6. When service is restored and what follow-up remains.

Use clear, factual language. Separate confirmed findings from assumptions.

## Continuous improvement

Review recurring incidents, manual tasks, capacity constraints and supplier issues. Agree a small number of improvement actions, assign owners and review progress at the next service meeting.
